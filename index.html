The Hardest Lessons

27 mistakes hardware founders make — and what each one costs.

Every one of them looked reasonable at the time. They are not technical failures. They are decisions made under pressure, with good intentions, in the belief that the open issue would be manageable next phase.

Drawn from The Hardest Hardware Lessons and The Hardest Factory Lessons by Hans Stam.

01Passing the gate under pressure

The mistake

Moving to the next phase with open issues that are “probably fine”, because investors, customers, competitors and a tired team all want the schedule kept.

What it costs

Each phase transition multiplies the cost of the same flaw by roughly 5 to 10 times. The fix itself doesn’t change. What changes is everything that has to be undone around it: the board re-spin, the certification re-test, the tooling modification, the production rework.

€2,000in prototyping
€20,000in EVT
€100,000in DVT
€500,000+in production

Order-of-magnitude illustration from the book, not a quotation.

The fix

Hold the gate on evidence, not on schedule. For every open item, write down what it costs to resolve here and what it is expected to cost in the next phase. “Probably fine” becomes a probability and a number.

02Choosing the platform that solves today’s problem

The mistake

Picking the hardware platform that is best for the product right now, without asking what it does to the product’s full commercial life.

What it costs

Problems that surface later in the supply chain, in certification and in the upgrade path, when the platform can no longer be changed.

The fix

Evaluate the platform against explicit criteria, document the evaluation, and treat the decision as one that will be reviewed in hindsight if supply chain, certification or versioning go wrong. Because it will be.

03Skipping the functional definition to move faster

The mistake

Starting to build before anyone has written down what the product is supposed to do.

What it costs

More time, not less. Prototype work is spent validating features and targets nobody agreed, or work that conflicts with a teammate’s assumptions about the product.

The fix

Write the functional definition and get it agreed. The time it takes is recovered many times over.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 2

04Developing form, function and feel one after another

The mistake

Treating the three prototype streams as sequential phases of one effort.

What it costs

Constraint conflicts between the streams are discovered late, when they are expensive to resolve.

The fix

Run them in parallel. The value is in the interactions between them, and those only show up when all three are developed together.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 2

05Treating a prototype that passes a demo as ready

The mistake

Concluding that P1 is ready for DFM handoff because it performs its main function on the bench.

What it costs

A demo only proves the product works under controlled conditions. It does not show how it behaves at the edges of its specification, in its target environments, or at acceptable manufacturing yield.

The fix

Judge P1 against the full functional definition, not the headline features.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 2

06Optimism at the DFM handoff

The mistake

Presenting the design as more complete and more resolved than it is, to make a strong impression or hit a date.

What it costs

You hand over the design and the unresolved risks hidden in it, into a phase where they cost far more to address.

The fix

Full transparency at handoff. Build an honest prototype, not an impressive one. Complete transparency is the sign of a mature development organisation, not of inadequate prototype work.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 2

Project set-up

The PRD, the TRD and the critical path are the operating system the programme runs on.

07Requirements two engineers can read differently

The mistake

Not writing requirements too loosely, but writing them so two engineers can read the same line and design to different specifications.

What it costs

The discrepancy stays invisible until the specifications meet in integration.

The fix

Have each requirement reviewed by the engineer who will design to it and the engineer who will test it, before it is baselined. If those two can’t agree what it means, it isn’t written correctly yet.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 3

08Skipping the TRD because the team is experienced

The mistake

Treating the technical requirements document as overhead when the design feels familiar.

What it costs

In EVT integration, two team leads turn out to have held different, equally reasonable mental models of a critical interface.

The fix

Write it. The TRD’s value isn’t recording what is in people’s heads; it is making that knowledge explicit, shared and agreed, so incompatibilities surface in a document review instead of an integration test.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 3

09Never naming the critical path

The mistake

Not identifying the critical path and not telling every team lead which tasks are on it.

What it costs

Leads prioritise what feels urgent — the nearest deadline, the loudest senior stakeholder — rather than what is critical. The programme misses its end date through many small slips nobody recognised as critical.

The fix

Identify the critical path explicitly and communicate it to everyone.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 3

EVT — Engineering Validation Test

EVT fails at the interfaces between teams, in the last two weeks, and at the gate.

10Coding before the software architecture is complete

The mistake

Starting to code while modules have vague responsibilities, interfaces are not fully specified, or state machines haven’t been checked against the use cases.

What it costs

Every question the architecture leaves open gets answered by integration test results in the last weeks of EVT, at about ten times the cost.

The fix

Close the architecture first.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 4

11Building test infrastructure when the product is ready to test

The mistake

Treating fixtures, test software, calibrated equipment and lab bookings as things that can be arranged afterwards.

What it costs

They all have lead time. The test programme is delayed by exactly as long as the infrastructure takes to build.

The fix

Start building test infrastructure on day one of EVT.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 4

12Releasing Gerbers before the DFM review and post-simulation

The mistake

Sending the board to fabrication because every day of delay is a day on the critical path.

What it costs

An unresolved DFM or signal-integrity problem means a re-spin: 4 to 8 weeks of critical path, many times what skipping the review saved.

The fix

Complete the DFM review. Complete the post-simulation. Then release.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 4

13Fixing the PCB outline before thermal and assembly analysis

The mistake

Committing the PCB outline before the thermal simulation and assembly analysis are done.

What it costs

Those analyses often force enclosure changes: a bigger heat sink, a repositioned component, a different mounting geometry. Each one hits the outline, and a board re-spin costs 4 to 8 weeks.

The fix

Run both analyses first. Committing early is a bet that they will confirm your first geometry.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 4

14Delivering DFM requirements after the designs are committed

The mistake

Letting the manufacturing team review what has already been decided, instead of shaping it.

What it costs

A DFM requirement that arrives before layout is complete costs nothing. After Gerbers are released it means a layout revision. After boards are built it means a production workaround or a re-spin.

The fix

Deliver DFM requirements at the beginning of EVT, before the engineering designs are committed.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 4

15A conditional EVT pass with “manageable” issues

The mistake

Passing the gate with open issues described as manageable in DVT, without saying what “manageable” means.

What it costs

Issues passed forward without a specific plan are the ones still open at the DVT gate, at five times the cost to resolve.

The fix

For every conditional item, define the resolution, the owner, the date, and which DVT activities depend on it. Without that, it is a pass in name only.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 4

DVT — Design Validation Test

The hardest lesson of DVT isn’t technical. It is holding the gate when the pressure to pass is highest.

16Treating certification as a step after engineering

The mistake

Planning certification for when the design is done.

What it costs

A failure caught in pre-compliance in week 2 of a 10-week DVT costs a design change and a re-test. The same failure caught at formal certification in week 8 costs that plus 4 to 8 weeks of laboratory re-scheduling on the critical path.

The fix

Plan and resource certification from the start of DVT, with pre-compliance testing front-loaded to the first weeks on the first hard-tooled hardware.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 5

17Releasing hard tooling before thermal and assembly analysis

The mistake

Committing to tooling under schedule pressure and deferring the two analyses most likely to change the enclosure.

What it costs

The thermal simulation takes 2 to 3 days. The assembly analysis, by a production assembler on the EVT mockup, takes 1 day. Together they cost under €5,000. A tooling modification driven by either finding costs €15,000 to €50,000.

The fix

Do the analysis before releasing the tooling.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 5

18Closing action items by documenting them

The mistake

Treating closure as paperwork when DVT is running late against a fixed PVT start date.

What it costs

Items marked closed that are only planned or initiated come back later, and at a higher price.

The fix

An action item is closed when the corrective action is implemented, verified on hardware and confirmed effective. Not before.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 5

19Passing the DVT gate when the evidence doesn’t support it

The mistake

Carrying open DVT items into PVT because the dates are fixed.

What it costs

The expected cost in PVT is usually 3 to 8 times higher, because PVT has no schedule buffer for problems DVT was supposed to resolve.

The fix

Hold the gate. Teams that do are the ones that run PVT on time and on budget.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 5

PVT — Production Validation Test

The same lesson as DVT, amplified.

20Passing PVT because the launch is three weeks away

The mistake

Moving into mass production with a pilot-run yield below target.

What it costs

A two-week PVT extension costs delayed revenue. A mass production failure costs delayed revenue plus rework, yield loss, potential recalls and customer relationship damage, all at once, with no development team standing by to help.

The fix

Hold the gate when the evidence requires it. The cost of holding it is always bounded. The cost of not holding it never is.

The Hardest Hardware Lessons — Chapter 6 and Conclusion

The factory

The factory is a mirror. It doesn’t add quality to your product; it reveals the quality of what you brought it.

21Treating the factory as a service

The mistake

Files in, goods out, everything in between somebody else’s business.

What it costs

The factory is an operation run by people, under a business model, on a calendar, with a power structure. Your biggest factory risk is being a low-priority customer and not knowing it, and being told only what the factory chooses to tell you.

The fix

Learn the operation. Walk the floor. Map the power. Know the calendar.

The Hardest Factory Lessons — Chapter 1

22Judging the factory by the tour

The mistake

Leaving impressed by the certificates, the running SMT line, the quiet operators and the defect rate on the slide.

What it costs

Everything you saw was real and almost none of it was information. You don’t know the true first-pass yield, the staff turnover, whether the inspector can hold a shipment on the last day of the month, or how that defect rate was counted.

The fix

Learn where to look: documents the tour doesn’t open, data the slide deck doesn’t show, and behaviour that only appears under pressure.

The Hardest Factory Lessons — Introduction

23Leaving the receiving dock to the factory’s defaults

The mistake

Never specifying what happens when components arrive.

What it costs

The factory’s defaults decide: inspection tuned to the factory’s risks, material review decisions made under schedule pressure without you in the room, traceability kept at whatever depth was convenient, and the door left open to parts that aren’t what the label says.

The fix

Put it on paper: inspection criteria by component class, a disposition framework for held material, traceability requirements, moisture-sensitive handling, and counterfeit controls. The dock is the cheapest place to find that a part is wrong.

The Hardest Factory Lessons — Chapter 2

24Trusting the warehouse database

The mistake

Assuming the records are the reality.

What it costs

The warehouse fails in silence and bills in arrears: the shortage the line finds on Wednesday that the records knew on Friday, the oldest reels clustered into your most stressed builds, and the excess-and-obsolete bill presented when you have the least leverage.

The fix

Check, in an afternoon: demand a clear-to-build, count ten locations, ask one picker which reel and why, and read one exposure report a month.

The Hardest Factory Lessons — Chapter 3

25Trusting the SMT yield you are shown

The mistake

Accepting the headline yield as the process’s real performance.

What it costs

The yield you’re shown is the yield after rework. A quiet AOI may mean tuning, not quality. The oven profile may be a memory of the one that was ratified. And short runs are disproportionately built from the line’s worst hours.

The fix

Demand the primary data, by stage, by defect, by location, and read it. Remember that half of every yield number was set months earlier, at your layout review.

The Hardest Factory Lessons — Chapter 4

26Not managing the tool you paid for

The mistake

Treating the molder as your supplier’s problem. You paid for the tool, the molder runs it, the CM marks it up, and none of you will be in the same room until something has gone wrong.

What it costs

A tool with an unread shot count is an outage with an unknown date. A cosmetic spec without boundary samples is an argument scheduled for your highest-volume month.

The fix

Get the disclosed address, an ownership plate, the shot count read quarterly, a process sheet with a press’s name on it, a mass balance that keeps regrind honest, boundary samples for cosmetics, and an annual half-day with the tool open on a bench.

The Hardest Factory Lessons — Chapter 5

27Underestimating assembly

The mistake

Hardware teams are trained on electronics and focused on SMT, so they consistently underestimate the area where the largest share of labour, cost and defect opportunities live.

What it costs

Every screw and cable in your design is paid for sixty times an hour, forever, and an assembly step that depends on operator skill becomes a quality dip when the workforce turns over.

The fix

Review the design for the line before it is frozen. The cheapest time to learn what the factory will cost is before it can no longer change what you send it.

The Hardest Factory Lessons — Introduction

Before you pass the next gate

Run it against where you actually are, not where you want to be.

  1. Which items can’t you check off with evidence? Each one is a cost you are carrying into the next phase.
  2. For each: is it cheaper to resolve now, or at the next phase?
  3. For every “manageable” issue: what is the resolution, who owns it, by when, and what depends on it?
  4. Is anyone on your team allowed to say the gate isn’t ready, and is that person rewarded for it?
